01 · Background

The question

Iowa controls wholesale liquor distribution and publishes every sale to licensed retailers as open data. That gives a full transaction record across several years, which is rare for public sales data.

I set out to answer three questions: how accurately can monthly sales be forecast, what caused the 2025 decline, and what should 2026 revenue look like?

The data comes from the Iowa Open Data Portal, one dataset per year: 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025.

Data preparation

  • Converted the yearly JSON downloads to CSV with Python
  • Loaded about 3M raw rows per year in Power Query
  • Removed duplicate rows
  • Grouped 50 category names into 8 groups
  • Summarized to category by month
02 · Data issues

What I had to fix before analyzing

The raw files had problems that would have changed the results if left alone. Each one was found by checking row counts and totals, not by looking at charts.

Empty full-year exports

The CSV download came back empty for the larger years.

FixDownloaded the JSON version in parts and converted it to CSV with a short Python script.

Duplicate rows

16–20% of rows were exact copies spread across the download files.

FixRemoved the file-name column so copies matched, then removed duplicates across all columns.

Invoice ID changed meaning

Starting September 2025, one invoice ID covered a whole order instead of a single line. Removing duplicates by invoice ID alone deleted about 90% of September–December sales.

FixRemoved duplicates on every column instead of invoice ID.

Commas inside fields

Store names and addresses with commas pushed values into the wrong columns, so a city name ("ANKENY") ended up in a number column and broke the load.

FixSwitched Power Query to quote-aware CSV parsing.

Reconciliation

CheckCompared yearly row counts and totals after cleaning. The 2025 total matched to the dollar: $424,814,286.

04 · Executive summary

Fewer bottles sold drove most of the decline

2025 sales were $424.8M, down $22.4M from 2024. About 60% of the drop came from selling fewer bottles and 39% from a lower average price. The mix between categories barely changed.

Methods I considered but didn't use

MethodWhy not
Moving averageIgnores seasonality, so it would miss the yearly peaks in October and December.
ARIMA / SARIMANeeds Python or R, works best with more than 60 monthly data points, and is hard to explain to non-technical readers.
Regression with driversThe dataset has no outside variables such as population or the economy, so drivers would have to be sourced and justified separately.
Machine learningRandom forest, XGBoost, or Prophet would likely overfit five years of monthly data, when a simple model already reaches 5–6% error.

Holt-Winters exponential smoothing is already covered: Excel's FORECAST.ETS is a version of it.

08 · Recommendations

What I would do next

RecommendationWhy
Plan 2026 around $403.8MThe decline lasted all of 2025, so a flat or growth budget is likely to miss.
Use seasonal naive as the baseline forecastLowest error in both years. Keep ETS as a cross-check.
Test a trend-adjusted seasonal naiveLast year's same month × (1 + recent year-over-year growth). Plain seasonal naive assumed no decline and overshot 2025 by 5.3%; adding the trend may close that gap.
Watch Whiskey and Vodka first60% of sales. A 5% change in Whiskey alone moves revenue by $8.1M.
Review Brandy and OtherBoth fell more than 9%. Brandy fell the fastest (−14.2%).
Stock ahead of October and DecemberThese are the yearly peaks. November had the largest miss in 2025.
Track bottle counts monthlyVolume caused most of the decline, so it is the earliest warning sign.
09 · Limitations

What this analysis can't tell you

  • Wholesale, not retail. The data is the state's sales to licensed retailers, not what consumers bought.
  • Seasonal naive repeats last year. It can't predict a change in trend, so it will miss a turnaround or a sharper decline.
  • No outside factors. Population, the economy, and competing products are not included.
  • Order timing. Sales are counted by order date, so an early or late order can shift dollars between months and affect single-month variances.
